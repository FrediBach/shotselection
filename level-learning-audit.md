# Does every level teach a small new thing?

Audit date: 2026-10-23. Reviewed repository revision: `26835ee`.

The course does **not yet meet this goal consistently**. It has a strong teaching framework—authored briefings, simulated alternatives, replays, result explanations and takeaways—but many levels reuse the same decision with a different layout, smaller target or stricter cut limit. Several lessons also describe an effect that the offered choices do not clearly demonstrate.

The assessment below covers **all 100 levels**, in course order. “New” means new relative to what the player has already encountered, including earlier chapters. A new application can qualify when it introduces one explicit relationship or decision rule. Extra balls, changed orientation, extra difficulty and a general instruction to combine existing skills do not qualify on their own. Practice and final assessments remain useful; the ratings ask whether they also satisfy the requested new-learning requirement.

## How to read the findings

| Assessment | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Meets** | A distinct, reasonably small learning increment is explained and supported by the available play. This is a design judgment, not proof that every player learns it. |
| **Partial** | A worthwhile increment exists, but the comparison, scaffolding or feedback does not make it clear enough. |
| **Not yet** | The level mainly repeats an earlier idea, adds difficulty without a new decision rule, or lacks the playable contrast needed for its stated lesson. |

Every level has its own finding and recommendation. Improvements for **Partial** and **Not yet** are proposed changes; they are not features currently implemented. Suggestions for **Meets** levels are optional refinements. Changes involving multiple shots, new position-region shapes or new diagnostic comparisons require engine/UI work as well as lesson text; the acceptance checks at the end describe how to validate the replacements.

## What was checked

This is a repository and simulation audit, not a player study or a claim of measured learning outcomes. It checks the lesson text against the authored layouts, candidate search, scoring requirements, visible controls and feedback. The game is about **selecting** strokes, so automatic aim and supplied stroke options are appropriate; the issue is whether choosing among them exercises the advertised new insight.

- [Course structure](src/engine/course.ts), [levels 1–90 and scenario generation](src/engine/scenarios.ts), and [levels 91–100](src/engine/broken-lessons.ts).
- [Candidate preparation](src/engine/preparation.ts), [objective evaluation](src/engine/objectives.ts), [ensemble analysis](src/engine/analysis.ts), and [table-defect physics](src/engine/table-conditions.ts).
- [Level briefing](src/components/LevelBriefing.tsx), [stroke/finish selection](src/components/FinishSelectionStep.tsx), [reveal and progression](src/App.tsx), and [shot explanations](src/lib/shot-feedback.ts).
- Existing curriculum, bank, plant, trick, objective and defect tests; deterministic runtime probes of prepared options for levels 1–60 and of plant ensembles for levels 61–70.

Runtime probes used `generateScenario(level, 0)`, `prepareRound(...)` and `simulateShot(...)`. Plant comparisons used 64 trials and the default character. The early cushion and mixed-pocket findings were additionally checked with seeds `0, 1, 5, 8`, which cover the four authored reflections. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, runtime observations refer to seed 0 and nominal strokes; they do not establish behavior for every perturbed trial or character.

The following targeted verification passed: **122 tests in six files**. These checks support the mechanical findings; they do not test pedagogical novelty.

## Findings that affect multiple levels

1. **Unique wording is not the same as a new lesson.** [The curriculum tests](src/engine/scenarios.test.ts) check unique titles, layouts, lessons, takeaways and focus text, plus feasibility and increasing precision. Those checks can pass while consecutive levels teach the same idea. The repeated “look at the whole route,” “leave the next ball available” and “choose margin” lessons need more specific incremental goals.

2. **The offered alternatives do not always demonstrate the headline.** The clearest example is level 7: its first-cushion lesson has no nominal cue-ball rebound in the four checked reflections. Level 4 mixes side-pocket and corner-pocket choices, complicating its advertised single side-pocket cut. Validate the actual candidates against each lesson's intended contrast, not just against general feasibility.

3. **Several causal explanations are confounded by simultaneous changes.** [Preparation](src/engine/preparation.ts) deliberately separates options by speed, spin and endpoint, and can also change pockets. This is useful for whole-shot selection, but comparing those options alone does not demonstrate the effect of spin in isolation. Use a short matched replay or an explicitly labeled before/after comparison when teaching a specific cause. Keep the normal whole-stroke comparison for the decision itself.

4. **Pattern planning is only partly represented.** Levels 32–40 fix ball 1 as the opener and ball 2 as the next ball. The engine checks availability of a next shot, but the player does not choose a sequence, play the second shot or evaluate position for a third ball. Level 31 has no next-ball objective at all. Either teach a narrower, accurately described two-ball skill or add the sequence decision needed by the chapter's promise.

5. **Traffic needs a more visible consequence.** For all 54 nominal options in the seed-0 probes of levels 32–40 and 42–50, the only ball contact was cue ball → ball 1. Removing spare balls from each settled position also left the number of permitted next-shot routes unchanged. This does not prove traffic is irrelevant to the initial search or perturbed trials; it shows that these nominal outcomes do not clearly demonstrate the advertised next-shot blockage or accidental-contact risks. Author a controlled example where the relevant blocker changes the available next shot and explain that change.

6. **Prediction and feedback could be more specific to the lesson.** Position and trick lessons hide the finish until commitment; ordinary, bank, plant and damaged-table lessons show finish markers. Existing feedback explains measured pot, scratch, finish and next-shot differences well, but it is largely generic rather than keyed to a level's new concept. Add one brief prediction or comparison tied to that concept, and answer it using the actual result. A revealed endpoint or a high score alone does not establish that the player understood the intended relationship.

7. **Completion measures participation, not understanding.** [The app](src/App.tsx) records completion after analysis regardless of score. That is a reasonable way to keep the course moving. Preserve it, but offer a short retry or corrective example when the specific new idea was missed. Avoid treating “lesson complete” as evidence of mastery.

8. **Late chapters contain both strong additions and repeated material.** The ordered contacts and distinct scoring paths in tricks 81–87, and the individual defect families in 91–97, offer concrete additions. Tournament 71–80 largely increases constraints on established position play. Tricks 88–89 return to cushion-first concepts from 51 and 57. The finale at 100 is an assessment of known faults unless a new small decision is deliberately added.

## Levels 1–10 — First strokes

Source: [authored lessons](src/engine/scenarios.ts), lines 29–153. For these direct-shot levels, preparation does supply draw, center-ball and follow: the three available longitudinal spins must differ pairwise. Their speeds also differ, and a triple can span different pockets ([preparation](src/engine/preparation.ts), lines 90–115 and 198–215). Thus these are comparisons of complete strokes, not controlled experiments in spin alone.

| Level / title | Intended learning increment | Assessment and evidence | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Your first pot | Treat the pot and cue-ball finish as one decision. | **Meets.** One object ball removes opener selection; three distinct finishes, visible speed/spin, replay and pot/position feedback support the first decision. Legal success excludes scratches. | Keep this narrow introduction; reveal terminology alongside the first relevant action. |
| 2 — Find the corner line | Read the contact line behind an object ball toward a corner. | **Meets.** This explicitly introduces contact-point geometry after the side-pocket introduction. The prepared approach line and actual corner-pot replay make it inspectable, although the seed-0 cut is only about 3.5°. | Optional refinement: annotate the ghost-ball contact point and object-to-pocket line once, then fade the annotation. |
| 3 — A longer direct pot | Understand that approach distance magnifies aim error. | **Partial.** The approach grows from roughly 0.52 m on level 1 to 0.77 m, with the same object-ball position. The simulation perturbs aim, but the player receives no matched short/long comparison identifying distance as the reason for a different margin. | Briefly compare the level-1 and level-3 approach with the same small angular offset; ask which creates more displacement at contact, then show it. |
| 4 — Meet the ball off-center | Separate the cue-ball approach from the outgoing object-ball and cue-ball paths. | **Partial.** A useful new emphasis, but the offered examples do not stay with the advertised side-pocket cut: at seed 0, A/B use the side pocket at about 14.1°, while follow C uses a corner at about 47.6° and adds rebounds. The pocket mismatch persists across the four tested reflections. | Author a triple that keeps the same side pocket and contact line; highlight the outgoing directions immediately after contact. Reserve pocket-changing comparisons for a later lesson. |
| 5 — Draw without guessing | Use draw as a route choice, rather than treating it as a softer stroke. | **Meets.** This is the first explicit draw-focused prediction task. The offered draw stroke is stronger but has a shorter cue-ball journey than center/follow at seed 0, supporting the warning that backspin and soft pace are different controls. | Optional refinement: mark the point of contact in replay so the shortened post-contact travel is easy to distinguish from the approach. |
| 6 — Follow beyond contact | Allow room for forward travel after contact. | **Partial.** The physical behavior exists, but the seed-0 follow option is much slower (0.82 m/s) and travels less overall (1.14 m) than center ball (1.60 m/s; 1.44 m). The supplied strokes do not clearly isolate the new follow lesson. | Add a same-speed, same-pocket center/follow demonstration before the scored choice, or explicitly explain why the slower follow stroke travels less in this particular set. |
| 7 — Read the first cushion | Continue predicting the cue-ball route after a rebound. | **Not yet.** In seeds 0, 1, 5 and 8, none of the three nominal cue-ball routes rebounds. A has one recorded cushion impact, attributable to the object ball; B/C have none. The named new mechanism is absent from the offered cue-ball examples. | Require at least one offered cue-ball route with a verified post-contact cushion rebound; pause the first replay at that rail and ask the player to predict the next direction. |
| 8 — Pace the diagonal | Budget speed for both potting and the remaining cue-ball travel. | **Partial.** The longer approach and different travel lengths make pace relevant, but every nominal direct option already pots, every finish has its own target, and speed changes together with spin. The costs of insufficient and excessive pace are not clearly isolated. | Use a short fixed-spin demonstration of too little, sufficient and excessive pace toward one common stopping area, then return to complete-stroke selection. |
| 9 — Keep the long pot simple | Distinguish side spin (English) from draw/follow. | **Meets.** This is the first explicit English lesson. Preparation requires exactly one nonzero-English option, the UI distinguishes lateral and vertical spin, and curriculum tests verify that English changes a nominal finish. | Optional refinement: rename the learning focus around English and show a same-stroke/no-English overlay; current title and focus emphasize already-known line and pace. |
| 10 — Let the cue ball travel | Combine line, pace, cut, English and rebounds. | **Not yet.** This is consolidation of levels 4–9: it keeps level 9's object position, moves the cue ball only 0.05 m, and increases English from 12 to 18. Moreover, the cue ball does not rebound in any offered nominal stroke in the four probed reflections; recorded cushion contacts belong to the object ball. | Give the recap one new distinction: running versus checking English on an actual cue-ball cushion contact, with matched zero/left/right-spin demonstrations and a verified rebound. |

## Levels 11–20 — Everyday pool

Source: [authored lessons](src/engine/scenarios.ts), lines 154–255. These levels genuinely add selectable balls: [curriculum tests](src/engine/scenarios.test.ts), line 117, require every object ball in levels 1–20 to be offered. The concern below is repeated decision criteria, rather than missing ball choices.

| Level / title | Intended learning increment | Assessment and evidence | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 — Two balls, one decision | Choose the opening ball as well as its finish. | **Meets.** The first two-ball layout adds a real decision dimension: both balls offer three strokes. Its nearer ball has the thinner cut in the seed-0 preparation (about 27.5° versus 8.5°), supporting the warning about choosing solely by proximity. | Keep this first comparison simple; optionally show both proposed contact lines together after the player chooses. |
| 12 — Distance or angle? | Trade distance against cut angle. | **Not yet.** Level 11 already explicitly asks for cut angles, distances and finishes, and warns that the closest ball can demand the thinner cut. A changed two-ball arrangement repeats that exact lesson. | Introduce the separate effect of **object-ball travel**: hold cut and cue approach approximately equal, vary the distance from object ball to pocket, and compare how the same contact error spreads at the pocket. |
| 13 — Compare the corner pots | Recognize that apparently symmetric pockets need not produce symmetric shots. | **Partial.** The asymmetry warning is a potentially distinct visual-reading lesson. Actual corner choices are very different in approach length (about 1.19 m versus 0.55 m at seed 0), however, so the exercise mostly repeats obvious distance/cut comparisons. | Make the corner layouts nearly symmetric and move only the cue ball off-center; ask the player to identify which contact line changes before comparing finishes. |
| 14 — Choose the cleaner angle | Favor fuller contact over the shorter-looking route. | **Not yet.** This is already the explicit lesson in levels 11–12. Its cue-ball position is identical to level 11 and both object balls shift only a few centimeters. | Teach a new kind of angle: pocket-entry angle. Contrast an oblique side-pocket entry with a cleaner corner entry, draw the usable mouth, and verify that the contrast survives preparation. |
| 15 — Read opposite ends | Scan the far half of the table before selecting a nearby ball. | **Not yet.** Comparing both balls was required at level 11; the side-versus-far-corner arrangement already appears in level 12. The existing two-ball selection does not create a new scanning skill. | Introduce a specific post-contact scratch line: arrange one otherwise attractive pot so its outgoing cue-ball direction runs toward a pocket, and ask the player to identify that danger before choosing. |
| 16 — Three ways to begin | Use a consistent angle → distance → cue-route comparison routine. | **Meets.** The first three-ball selection gives the player a larger real choice, and the lesson supplies a small procedural addition: applying the same ordered checks to each candidate. | Optional refinement: let players mark each candidate as checked, then fade this aid on later levels. |
| 17 — Favor a calm finish | Prefer a controlled amount of post-contact travel. | **Not yet.** Finishing travel is already part of levels 1, 6, 8, 11 and the immediately preceding three-ball routine. The new layout does not identify another factor beyond those earlier comparisons. | Introduce the cost of an **avoidable cushion contact**: compare otherwise similar pots where one cue route stops before the rail and one rebounds, then inspect the resulting variation. |
| 18 — Distance with a choice | Apply earlier comparisons when all approaches become longer. | **Not yet.** The lesson explicitly calls this a test of earlier comparisons; it moves the cue ball deeper while preserving level 17's object-ball positions. More distance increases difficulty without adding a decision rule. | Teach how a thinner cut transfers less of the cue ball's motion to the object ball. Use comparable travel distances, contrasting cuts, and a contact-speed overlay to connect angle to the pace required for the pot. |
| 19 — Shortlist four open balls | Reduce a larger candidate set to two promising options. | **Partial.** The fourth ball and explicit shortlisting strategy are new, but the interface accepts only one selected ball and does not record or compare a two-ball shortlist. English is also reintroduced even though it was taught at level 9. | Add a temporary two-candidate comparison step with their contact lines and strokes side by side; keep familiar spin settings while teaching the shortlist. |
| 20 — Choose the comfortable stroke | Select a repeatable whole stroke from the shortlist. | **Not yet.** The same four object balls remain, the cue ball moves only 0.03 m, and the choice again combines familiar pace, spin and finish repeatability. | Give this recap one new judgment: recognizing a near tie. Present two genuinely close options, explain the sampling uncertainty already acknowledged in feedback, and ask when their difference is too small to justify a confident preference. |

## Levels 21–30 — Play for position

Source: [authored lessons](src/engine/scenarios.ts), lines 256–386. The shared zone and next-shot constraints are implemented, not merely narrated: [objectives](src/engine/objectives.ts), lines 52–76 and 100–118, check settled balls, clear routes and the permitted cut, while preparation proves at least one nominal next pot. Individual nominal endpoints are hidden before commitment for these levels ([PoolTable](src/components/PoolTable.tsx), lines 476–492). The zone is generated from a qualifying offered finish, rather than independently authored for each stated sublesson ([preparation](src/engine/preparation.ts), line 215).

| Level / title | Intended learning increment | Assessment and evidence | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21 — Leave a shot on the 2 | Aim for a shared landing area that leaves the next ball playable. | **Meets.** This introduces a real new objective: pot 1, hold a common zone, and preserve a legal next shot on 2. Outcome hiding makes the stroke choice predictive, and the engine verifies the next route. | Optional refinement: keep all three introductory strokes aimed at the same pocket; seed-0 preparation currently gives the qualifying follow stroke a different pocket from the other two. |
| 22 — Control the length | Avoid finishing short or long of the zone. | **Not yet.** Zone control and reading pace/spin were already required at level 21. The radius changes by only 0.002 m; that is a tighter demand, not an additional concept. | Introduce an explicit stop-shot calibration: distinguish a sliding cue ball at impact from one that has acquired forward roll, using a nearly full contact and visible contact-time state. |
| 23 — Find the middle of the zone | Prefer a finish with margin from the boundary. | **Partial.** Center margin is a useful refinement, and scoring rewards target closeness. At seed 0, however, only one option is nominally inside the zone; the others are about 0.37 m and 1.10 m from its center versus a 0.196 m radius. That is center-versus-miss, not center-versus-edge. | Require two legal, next-shot-preserving finishes inside the same zone—one central and one near its edge—then compare the fraction that remain inside under the same errors. |
| 24 — Bring position back across | Work backward from the next shot instead of reusing a familiar spin choice. | **Partial.** Backward planning is a useful procedural emphasis, but merely reversing the layout is not new: all levels after 1 already permit seeded X/Y reflections. The existing choice does not reveal whether the player actually starts from ball 2. | First ask which side/contact point makes ball 2 playable; reveal its compatible landing area, then ask which opening stroke reaches it. Fade that sequence later. |
| 25 — Leave the useful angle | Judge the next-ball angle rather than closeness alone. | **Partial.** The cut limit is genuinely enforced, but it was already present at level 21. At seed 0 all three nominal endpoints leave a permitted next shot (best available cuts roughly 11°, 32° and 47°, all below 62°), so the new angle trap is weakly isolated. | Put two nominal finishes inside the zone: the nearer one must leave only an excessive cut, while the farther one leaves a legal cut. Draw those two next-shot angles in the explanation. |
| 26 — Leave space to approach | Preserve access to the next ball's contact point, not merely a view of its center. | **Partial.** The engine tests ghost-ball access, so the concept has mechanical support. In the seed-0 set, all three nominal finishes still leave valid next routes; none demonstrates the stated nearly-on-top-of-ball-2 trap. | Author a close finish that can see ball 2 but cannot reach the required contact point, alongside a slightly more distant legal finish; visualize the ghost-ball clearance after selection. |
| 27 — Match spin to the stopping point | Choose draw/center/follow for a defined landing area. | **Not yet.** Levels 5–6 introduced those controls and levels 21–22 already apply them to the zone. A smaller zone and changed approach repeat the skill; at seed 0 follow additionally switches the potting pocket. | Introduce the decay of draw over the approach: show launch spin versus spin at contact for matched short/long approaches, then ask when more initial backspin is necessary to retain draw. |
| 28 — Control the deeper approach | Include speed lost before the first contact in the position plan. | **Not yet.** Speed budgeting over a long approach was introduced at level 8 and revisited at 18; combining it with the existing zone is practice. The layout does create a next-angle failure for one seed-0 option, but the briefing does not teach that as its increment. | Use that existing distinction: teach overshooting the useful side of ball 2. Make the angle failure visible independently of zone failure, and explain why a longer cue-ball route can lose the next shot even when the first pot works. |
| 29 — Hold a useful side | Respect the next-shot cut limit while considering English. | **Not yet.** English is familiar from 9–10 and 19–20, and next-angle limits from 21 onward. All seed-0 nominal options have an allowed next shot; adding English and tightening the zone mainly bundles earlier material. | Introduce a **signed** next-shot angle: two finishes should both satisfy the present cut limit, but only one side of ball 2 should naturally lead toward a small preview target for ball 3. Keep the new check to selecting the useful side, not executing a full run. |
| 30 — Complete the two-ball plan | Combine the first pot, route, zone and next-ball access. | **Not yet.** Its stated role is a recap; those same checks have been required since level 21. A 0.182 m zone, 59° limit and an English option add demands rather than a new idea. | Add one small verification step: let the player select the intended pocket for ball 2 before committing to ball 1, then show whether the chosen finish supports that particular next shot rather than merely any legal route. |

## Levels 31–40 — Build a pattern

Evidence: authored lessons in [scenarios.ts](src/engine/scenarios.ts), lines 379–500; option/zone preparation in [preparation.ts](src/engine/preparation.ts), especially lines 118–130, 214–227 and 231–258; next-shot checks in [objectives.ts](src/engine/objectives.ts), lines 29–75 and 101–119; reserved-ball UI in [BallSelectionStep.tsx](src/components/BallSelectionStep.tsx). Level 31 has no next-shot objective. Levels 32–40 allow only ball 1 as the opener and reserve ball 2; they do not let the player choose or execute a multishot order. Their zone is generated at a qualifying offered stroke's endpoint, with a simulated proof that ball 2 can then be potted. This is useful position training, but weaker than the chapter's promise to choose a pattern.

| Level / title | Intended learning increment | Assessment and evidence | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 — Read the whole table | Choose an opener for access to a later ball. | **Not yet.** Four-ball scanning repeats levels 19–20. Unlike levels 21–30, this level has no next-ball requirement: scoring tests each opener's pot and its own finish, without rewarding the claimed connection. | Ask the player to nominate a next ball, then evaluate access to that nominated ball after the opener. Offer one easy opener that loses that connection. |
| 32 — Connect the first two | Work backward from the second shot. | **Not yet.** Working backward and the fixed 1→2 zone task already occur in levels 21–30, particularly 24. A third object ball and a 0.180 m radius add constraints, but no new sequence decision. | Extend the exercise through actually selecting and playing the second shot from the settled first-shot layout. | 33 — Reserve the second ball | Preserve a chosen second ball. | **Not yet.** Ball 2 is already reserved in level 21 and in level 32's warning. | 34 — Leave the layout readable | Account for remaining balls while planning. | **Not yet.** Adding a fourth object ball repeats whole-table scanning from 31 and traffic awareness from 32–33. | 39 — Choose a sequence with margin | Favor a connection robust to small stroke errors. | **Not yet.** Ensemble reliability, English versus no English, and zone/next-shot checks all appear earlier, including 23 and 29–30. The player still chooses one stroke on ball 1, not between sequences. | Compare two playable two-shot orders and their combined completion rate. Give one the easier opening pot but a substantially weaker second shot, making sequence reliability the new decision. |
| 40 — Keep the next lane open | Check both parts of the next shot with English included. | **Not yet.** This combines the previous lane checks with English already practiced in 39. The seed-0 failed next-shot option remains invalid with spare balls removed and becomes playable under a relaxed cut limit, so its failure demonstrates angle rather than the claimed blockage. | Teach a fallback plan: choose between a finish leaving only one narrow continuation and a finish preserving two viable next balls; evaluate continuation availability explicitly. |

## Levels 41–50 — Navigate traffic

Evidence: [scenarios.ts](src/engine/scenarios.ts), lines 501–622; swept cue-ball and object-ball corridors in [objectives.ts](src/engine/objectives.ts), lines 29–57; nominal feasibility filtering in [preparation.ts](src/engine/preparation.ts); measured post-shot feedback in [shot-feedback.ts](src/lib/shot-feedback.ts). These lessons have real collision physics and real final-layout checks. The weakness is that their authored teaching contrast often is not assured: putting more balls on the table does not itself demonstrate a new kind of obstruction. Levels 42–50 continue the same fixed 1→2 task, with radii decreasing from 0.162 m to 0.146 m.

| Level / title | Intended learning increment | Assessment and evidence | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 41 — Find the open corridor | Judge a gap using the full ball width. | **Partial.** Full-width clearance is a useful explicit refinement, and route preparation checks swept corridors. However, blocked candidates can simply be unavailable with the generic explanation that no three playable finishes were found; the player is not shown why the apparent gap fails. | Offer or illustrate a center line that looks clear but lacks ball-width clearance, alongside a clearly wide corridor. After the choice, show the swept ball width and the obstruction. |
| 42 — Position through traffic | Preserve next-shot access after passing other balls. | **Not yet.** This combines requirements already present in 32–40. In seed 0, all three nominal strokes leave a next shot; unsuccessful options miss the zone, rather than demonstrating lost access through traffic. | Introduce moving traffic: arrange one finish that displaces a spare ball into the next-shot corridor and one that leaves it clear. Explain that the final positions, not the starting diagram, determine access. |
| 43 — Pass the nearby ball | Anticipate a glancing collision after the pot. | **Partial.** This can isolate a useful post-contact hazard. The three seed-0 nominal options make no secondary contact, though near misses could still matter under perturbation; the lesson does not guarantee a visible graze-versus-clear comparison. | Place one spare ball beside the post-contact route and offer a clear stroke plus a stroke that visibly grazes it. Use matched comparisons where possible and identify the resulting cue-ball deflection. |
| 44 — Protect both pocket lines | Check the first and second object-ball routes. | **Not yet.** Both halves of a next shot were already discussed in 37 and 40. The first pot is prevalidated for every offered stroke; in the seed-0 sample the next-shot failures are cut-limit failures even after removing traffic. | Extend the proposed level 30's declared next pocket by teaching when traffic forces a change of pocket: show a blocked preferred route for ball 2 and a clear alternative that needs a different first-shot finish. Explain the blocker that makes the original plan unusable. |
| 45 — Read the crowded half | Find useful space around the next ball. | **Not yet.** More balls and a smaller zone repeat the scanning, screening and contact-access ideas. No additional objective separates this crowded half from the earlier layouts. | Introduce a pocketed ball's effect on the layout: compare removing a ball that opens a corridor with taking another pot that leaves the corridor blocked. Verify the newly opened route. |
| 46 — Avoid borrowing a collision | Distinguish reliable clearance from a lucky helping nudge. | **Partial.** Sensitivity to a secondary collision is a specific refinement of avoiding contact. None of the seed-0 nominal strokes actually relies on such a nudge, and the option generator does not require this contrast. | Include a collision-assisted nominal success and a clean nominal success. Show their perturbed collision frequency and success rates so the learner can see why the apparent help is brittle. |
| 47 — Keep the return lane clear | Recheck traffic after a cushion rebound. | **Partial.** Extending clearance checks to a returning route is a meaningful small addition. Seed-0 option C does rebound and reaches the zone, but no nominal option contacts a spare ball; the others fail the existing zone/angle checks. | Place a single obstacle on the return leg and contrast two rebound routes with similar destinations, one with clearance and one with a graze risk. Mark the return leg in feedback. |
| 48 — Find the usable gap | Combine gap width with the next cut angle. | **Not yet.** Width was explicit in 41 and 43, and the cut limit has existed since 21. ## Recommended order of improvement

1. **Fix mismatches between the lesson and actual choices first.** Start with level 7's missing cue-ball rebound, level 4's mixed pocket comparison, and the pattern/traffic claims. These require checking or changing the prepared candidates and feedback, not just rewriting titles.
2. **Give the repeated middle levels distinct decisions.** Rework the repeated angle/distance comparisons in 11–20, two-ball position tasks in 24–50 and tournament tasks in 71–80 using the per-level suggestions. Keep earlier skills in use while making one new relationship explicit.
3. **Make new physics effects easy to compare.** Add focused replay annotations or matched comparisons for spin, cushion losses, contact chains and defects. Do not claim that changing spin alone caused an advantage when pace or pocket also changed.
4. **Keep the final challenges and add a small discovery.** For repeated or combined lessons such as 88–89 and 99–100, retain their challenge while adding the specific new decision described in the table. If the design instead keeps them purely as reviews, label them honestly as review/assessment and acknowledge that this relaxes the “every level teaches something new” requirement.

## A practical acceptance check for each revised level

Before considering a level complete, be able to fill in: **“The player already knows ___; this level adds ___; the choice that reveals it is ___; the result shows it by ___.”**

- Give the level one primary learning increment and name its prerequisite, ideally with explicit concept identifiers in the lesson data. A shorter target radius or altered layout is evidence of difficulty, not a sufficient increment.
- Ensure at least two offered choices produce an instructive contrast involving that increment. The alternative need not be a guaranteed failure; a difference in tolerance, route or preserved next shot can be enough.
- Verify that contrast in actual prepared scenarios across the authored reflections and supported characters. Do not require the same winning stroke for every character: execution strengths can legitimately change the ranking.
- Make feedback identify the relevant event, path segment or tradeoff in the player's result. When the claimed mechanism does not occur, do not present a canned takeaway as if it did.
- Use a short prediction, explanation choice or replay comparison to check understanding. Keep advancement available, with an optional retry when useful.

The report recommends changes only. No gameplay, lesson definitions or scoring code was changed by this audit.

## Implementation progress — stage 1 (2026-10-24)

The findings above describe the original audit revision. Implementation is now staged in the recommended order; the original ratings have not been reclassified as a completed curriculum redesign.

- **Level 4:** preparation now requires one side pocket and one contact line for all three strokes. A denser speed search retains draw, center and follow with distinct finishes. - **Level 7:** the simulator records cue-ball cushion events separately from object-ball impacts, including time, position, incoming/outgoing velocity and whether contact has occurred.
